# Chapter 1: The Toyota Way 2001

In 2001 Toyota published an internal document called "The Toyota Way 2001". It was written to explain the company's culture to a fast-growing, increasingly international workforce, at a time when more and more Toyota vehicles were being built outside Japan. The document was issued under president Fujio Cho.

The Toyota Way 2001 rests on two pillars: Continuous Improvement and Respect for People.

Under Continuous Improvement there are three values:

- Challenge: forming a long-term vision and meeting challenges with courage and creativity.
- Kaizen: improving business operations continuously, always driving for innovation and evolution.
- Genchi Genbutsu: going to the source to find the facts, so as to make correct decisions, build consensus and achieve goals.

Under Respect for People there are two values:

- Respect: respecting others, making every effort to understand each other, taking responsibility and doing one's best to build mutual trust.
- Teamwork: stimulating personal and professional growth, sharing opportunities for development and maximising individual and team performance.

Respect for people at Toyota does not mean being soft. It means respecting people's ability to think and to solve problems, and therefore giving them challenging work and holding high expectations. Respect also extends to suppliers, dealers, customers and the communities in which Toyota operates.

# Chapter 2: Liker's Fourteen Principles

Jeffrey K. Liker, a professor of industrial and operations engineering at the University of Michigan, published *The Toyota Way: 14 Management Principles from the World's Greatest Manufacturer* in 2004. Liker grouped fourteen principles into four sections, sometimes called the 4P model: Philosophy, Process, People and Partners, and Problem Solving. The summaries below paraphrase the principles.

## Section I: Long-term philosophy

Principle 1. Base management decisions on a long-term philosophy, even at the expense of short-term financial goals. The company should have a sense of purpose larger than making money, and should generate value for the customer, society and the economy.

## Section II: The right process will produce the right results

Principle 2. Create continuous process flow to bring problems to the surface.

Principle 3. Use pull systems to avoid overproduction.

Principle 4. Level out the workload (heijunka). Work like the tortoise, not the hare.

Principle 5. Build a culture of stopping to fix problems, to get quality right the first time.

Principle 6. Standardised tasks are the foundation for continuous improvement and employee empowerment.

Principle 7. Use visual control so no problems are hidden.

Principle 8. Use only reliable, thoroughly tested technology that serves your people and processes.

## Section III: Add value to the organisation by developing people and partners

Principle 9. Grow leaders who thoroughly understand the work, live the philosophy and teach it to others.

Principle 10. Develop exceptional people and teams who follow the company's philosophy.

Principle 11. Respect your extended network of partners and suppliers by challenging them and helping them improve.

## Section IV: Continuously solving root problems drives organisational learning

Principle 12. Go and see for yourself to thoroughly understand the situation (genchi genbutsu).

Principle 13. Make decisions slowly by consensus, thoroughly considering all options; implement decisions rapidly. In Japanese this consensus-building process is called nemawashi.

Principle 14. Become a learning organisation through relentless reflection (hansei) and continuous improvement (kaizen).

Liker argued that most companies trying to copy Toyota focus only on Section II, the process tools, and neglect the philosophy, the development of people and the problem-solving culture. He called this the reason why many lean programmes produce only short-term gains.

# Chapter 3: Visual Management and the 5S System

Visual control means designing the workplace so that anyone can tell at a glance whether work is normal or abnormal. Examples include andon boards, painted floor markings for where materials belong, shadow boards that outline each tool, and production-status boards that compare actual output with the plan hour by hour.

5S is a workplace organisation method that supports visual control. The five steps are:

1. Seiri (Sort): remove items that are not needed.
2. Seiton (Set in order): arrange the necessary items so they are easy to find and use.
3. Seiso (Shine): clean the workplace and inspect equipment while cleaning.
4. Seiketsu (Standardise): create standards to keep the first three S's in place.
5. Shitsuke (Sustain): build the discipline and habits to maintain the system.

# Chapter 4: Developing Leaders and Teams

Toyota organises production into small groups. A team leader typically supports a handful of team members, and a group leader supervises several teams. Team leaders answer andon calls, fill in for absent workers, train new members and lead kaizen activities. This structure means problems are dealt with close to where they occur.

Toyota leaders are expected to be teachers. Training often follows the Training Within Industry (TWI) method, originally developed in the United States during the Second World War, with its job instruction steps: prepare the worker, present the operation, try out the performance and follow up.

Mentoring at Toyota is often described as a sensei relationship. A manager rarely gives a subordinate the answer. Instead the manager asks questions and sends the person back to the gemba to observe more closely. This approach develops the subordinate's own problem-solving ability.

# Chapter 5: Hansei and Nemawashi

Hansei means self-reflection. Even after a successful project, Toyota teams hold a hansei meeting to identify what did not go well and what could be improved next time. Saying "there were no problems" is regarded as itself a problem, because it suggests the team was not looking hard enough.

Nemawashi literally refers to preparing the roots of a tree before transplanting it. In business it means quietly laying the groundwork for a decision by discussing it with everyone affected, collecting their input and building agreement before the formal decision meeting. Decisions can therefore look slow from the outside, but implementation tends to be fast because people have already agreed.

# Chapter 6: Supplier Partnerships

Toyota is known for long-term relationships with a relatively small number of suppliers. Many Japanese suppliers belong to supplier associations, and Toyota helps them apply TPS. At the same time, Toyota sets demanding targets for cost reduction and quality. Researchers have described the relationship as a combination of close cooperation and constant challenge.

The Aisin fire of February 1997 is a famous example of the strength of this network. A fire destroyed the main plant of Aisin Seiki that made P-valves, a brake component used in almost all Toyota vehicles. Because Toyota held only a few days of inventory, its assembly plants had to stop. Within days, dozens of other companies in the Toyota group and supplier network, many of which had never made P-valves before, set up improvised production. Toyota's plants restarted within about a week.
